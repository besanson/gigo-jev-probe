1. **Critical — S1’s unsafe-exposure bound is false as stated.** **[§1, definition of \(e_i^+\); §3 S1; §10.5]**  
   Let \(R=\{0,1\}^2\), \(C=F_s=\{f_1,f_2\}\), and allow iff \(f_1\land f_2\). The true tuple is `(false, false)`; with probability \(q>0\), both sensors admit `true`, otherwise both are correct. Changing either field alone leaves the verdict deny, so the registered definition gives \(e_1^+=e_2^+=0\). Changing both produces allow with probability \(q\). Thus \(P(\text{deny}\to\text{allow})=q>0=\sum e_i^+\). Sufficiency and even monotonicity of the Boolean contract do not prevent this. The ordinary \(e_i+u_i\) bound survives; the directional refinement does not. Consequently, §10.5’s assertion that a test-split empirical bound necessarily holds is true only for the ordinary bound. S3 also requires clarification of whether “deny-ward” means globally non-increasing across contexts or merely harmless under the registered single-field substitution.

2. **Critical — H1 tests a random plug-in estimate, not the registered population bound.** **[§5.4 metrics; §6 H1; §10.5]**  
   Freezing validation estimates does not make them population upper bounds. Consider one sensed field whose errors always change the verdict, with true error probability \(0.002\), no abstention, and a fixed admission policy. S1 holds with equality. Nevertheless, the probability of zero errors in 300 validation items and at least one error in 700 test items is approximately **41.3%**. That produces estimated \(B=0\) and a binomial p-value of zero, surviving both multiplicity corrections. H1 can therefore announce “bound violated” when the mathematical bound is correct. Reusing validation data to select thresholds and estimate their error rates adds selection optimism. The registered test cannot distinguish theorem failure from estimation error.

3. **High — H2 treats repeated observations of the same items as independent pairs.** **[§5.5; §6 H2; §10.6–7]**  
   The 2,800 pairs arise from 700 underlying test items evaluated across two arms and two sensors. Shared truth, difficulty and document content create dependence across those four observations. An ordinary exact McNemar test over the pooled pairs does not account for that clustering. “Paired item bootstrap” does not specify whether all four observations of an underlying item remain together during resampling. Holm correction does not repair the within-hypothesis dependence problem. Pooling also permits an aggregate improvement to conceal deterioration for one sensor or arm.

6. **High — The selection objectives do not generally minimise the claimed exposure bound.** **[NOVELTY.md, “selects contracts to minimise it”; §3 S2; §5.5; §10.4]**  
   S2’s deletion argument works for nested sensed-field sets with unchanged retained-field error rates. It does not establish that fewer sensed fields means a smaller bound across arbitrary reducts: one field with \(e+u=0.20\) is worse than two whose combined contribution is \(0.002\). The alternative objective \(\sum e_i\) omits abstention entirely; an always-unknown sensor has zero admitted-error cost. E2 avoids these trade-offs by comparing a shared sensed approval field against that same field plus one additional sensed field. Its selected reduct is fixed before observing either sensor. It tests that particular substitution of truthful recorded information for sensing, not general exposure-minimising contract selection.

7. **High — “Approval validity” can delegate an authority subdecision to the sensor.** **[§1–2; §5.1 approval questions; §5.4 approval banks]**  
   The sensor is asked whether approval is **valid/current**, while the positive statements establish only that someone granted or recorded approval. The registered inputs do not establish the approver’s authority, applicable scope, revocation status or an expiration rule. If `valid` means actual authority validity, the model supplies a policy-relevant judgment that the deterministic gate subsequently trusts. If it means only that the document asserts approval, the experiment does not test actual approval validity. Never emitting the final allow/deny token establishes interface separation, but does not resolve this semantic delegation.

8. **High — The 1% admission ceiling is neither a population guarantee nor a contract-level error ceiling.** **[§5.2; §10.1, §10.5]**  
   Thresholds satisfy an empirical, per-candidate constraint on the same validation data used to choose them. No finite-sample confidence guarantee is registered. Pooling three correlated noise variants also does not establish the ceiling separately at each noise level. Finally, classwise false-positive rates are different quantities from wrong-admission probability across multiple candidate values, error conditional on admission, and unsafe contract-verdict probability. The number “1%” therefore cannot bear those stronger interpretations.

9. **Medium — H3’s scientific conclusion is built into its generator.** **[§3 N6; §5.7; §6 H3]**  
   E4 generates precisely one potentially erroneous false field per item, flips it with probability 0.05, and defines every such flip to cause unsafe allowance. The population unsafe rate is consequently 0.05 by construction. TOST can fail through sampling variation or an implementation defect, but there is no independent scientific alternative for it to discover. E4 is a constructive witness and implementation check; “Supported: attained” does not supply independent empirical evidence for the general bound. Its disjoint-error construction also excludes the interaction that falsifies S1 in finding 1.

10. **Medium — E3 contains definitional outcomes and changes the exposure denominator.** **[§5.6]**  
    Under `unknown → deny`, every item receives allow or deny, so registered decision coverage is identically 100%, regardless of \(\varepsilon\). Changing unknown outcomes to escalation cannot change the set of unsafe allowances: unknown items were previously denied. Unsafe rate is therefore unchanged if measured over all items; if measured only over decided items, its denominator changes. Exposure explicitly uses the latter denominator, so differences between deny and escalate partly reflect removing difficult items from evaluation. The unsafe-rate denominator is not specified.

11. **Medium — The calibration target differs from the probability requested from the model.** **[§5.1 prompts; §5.4 missing-noise rule; §5.8 E5]**  
    The prompts request the probability that **the record shows** a value. Evaluation uses the underlying tuple’s value even when the relevant statement has been removed. For a missing statement, assigning low probability to every “record shows value \(v\)” proposition can be semantically correct, while the registered labels still mark one candidate positive. Calibration rejection can therefore reflect a target mismatch rather than miscalibration. Contradictory evidence introduces a related distinction between what is asserted and what is true.

12. **Medium — The synthetic design limits what the results establish about text-only authority evidence.** **[§5.4–5.5; §10.2–3, §10.7]**  
    Observation cost and source tier do not establish that a field exists only in unstructured evidence. The experiments then render a few explicit templates, remove surrounding operational context, and place contradictions in a consistently marked appended comment. Validation and test use the same phrase banks and corruption mechanism. This measures performance within that controlled generator, including its positional and source cues; it does not test unseen document forms, competing authoritative sources, or temporal reconciliation. Removing recorded fields prevents one shortcut, but does not demonstrate that the remaining sensed fields cannot predict each other through domain correlations.
